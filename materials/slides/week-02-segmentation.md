# Week 2: Segmentation Concepts and Case

## Marketing Principle #1: All customers differ

Customer heterogeneity is variation among customers in their needs, preferences, constraints, purchase behavior, and responses to marketing activity. A difference becomes strategically important when it changes what the firm should offer or do.

## Today’s questions

- Why does customer heterogeneity create a strategic problem?
- Which differences should define market segments?
- How do segmentation, targeting, and positioning work together?
- How does customer centricity support ongoing learning?
- Which DentMax segment should receive priority?

## Five sources of heterogeneity

1. Individual differences in resources, abilities, tastes, and constraints.
2. Life experiences that shape expectations and habits.
3. Functional needs that cause customers to use the same category differently.
4. Self-identity, status, values, and belonging.
5. Marketing activity that influences which benefits customers notice and value.

## Latent customer heterogeneity

Different preferences may exist even when current choices do not reveal them. Legal, economic, technological, or innovation constraints can prevent customers from expressing what they want.

## Sears and AT&T

Sears illustrates how specialists can remove attractive niches from a broad-market business. AT&T illustrates how deregulation and competition can make previously hidden differences visible.

## Evolution of segmentation

- Mass marketing emphasizes scale with one broad offer.
- Niche marketing designs specialized offers for narrower groups.
- One-to-one marketing uses customer-level information to personalize decisions.

The recurring trade-off is customer fit versus the cost and complexity of customization.

## Spotify and personalization

Listening history reveals individual preferences, supports personalized discovery, and helps advertisers reach narrower audiences. Personalization creates value when additional relevance exceeds data and implementation costs.

## STP

### Segmenting

Divide the market into groups whose members have similar needs and whose needs differ across groups.

### Targeting

Select groups where the firm can create and capture value better than competitors.

### Positioning

Build a clear relative advantage in the minds of the chosen customers.

## Needs and descriptors

Needs and benefits should define segments. Age, income, location, company size, channel, and observed behavior can help identify and reach members. A demographic category is not automatically a meaningful segment.

## Segmentation research process

1. Define the category and relevant population.
2. Use qualitative research to identify purchase attributes.
3. Survey a relevant sample about needs and benefits.
4. Combine related questions when necessary.
5. Group customers by similar need patterns.
6. Profile, name, and assess the groups.

## Useful segment profiles

A useful profile explains distinctive needs, segment size, growth, willingness to pay, expected economics, observable descriptors, and the marketing action that should change.

## Targeting and the 3Cs

- Customers: need intensity, size, growth, willingness to pay, and service requirements.
- Company: capabilities, resources, brand meaning, channels, and economics.
- Competitors: relative strengths, likely responses, and unserved needs.

## Six target-selection criteria

A target should reflect a meaningful need, differ from other groups, fit the firm’s capabilities, remain sustainable, be identifiable and reachable, and offer positive long-term value.

## GE matrix

The matrix compares market attractiveness with competitive strength. Bubble size can represent segment size or economic value. The framework organizes judgment but does not replace assumptions about weights or data quality.

## Positioning

Positioning defines the relative advantage the firm wants target customers to associate with its offer. The firm can change the actual offer and customer perceptions. Every element of the marketing program should support the intended position.

## Positioning statement

For **[target customer]**, our offer provides **[benefit]** because **[credible support for relative advantage]**.

## Customer centricity

A customer-centric organization places customer needs at the center of strategy, structure, information flows, and performance measures. Market orientation requires intelligence generation, intelligence dissemination, and responsiveness.

## DentMax case

DentMax combined trade-show RFID behavior, prior purchases, product needs, and trusted information sources. Cluster analysis produced four groups:

| Segment | Share | Priorities | Initial implication |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Loyalists | 12% | Image quality and diagnostic flexibility | Strong fit with established strengths |
| Switchables | 22% | Software integration and technical assistance | Potential source of share growth |
| Generalists | 49% | Price and technical assistance | Large but inconsistent with a premium position |
| Apathetics | 17% | Low involvement | Limited reason for specialized investment |

## DentMax discussion

1. Which segment should DentMax prioritize?
2. Which need should anchor the decision?
3. Where does DentMax have a sustainable relative advantage?
4. What additional evidence is needed?
5. Write a positioning statement.

## Recap

- Needs should define segments; descriptors help identify and reach members.
- Targeting combines market attractiveness with competitive strength.
- Positioning specifies a credible relative advantage for a chosen target.
- Customer centricity supports ongoing learning after the initial STP analysis.
