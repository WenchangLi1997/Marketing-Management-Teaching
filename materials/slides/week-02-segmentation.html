Marketing Principle #1

All customers differ

Customers vary in what they need, what they value, how they choose, and how they respond to marketing activity.

The managerial task is to identify the differences that should change the firm's decisions.

Sources of heterogeneity

Why customer preferences differ

Individual differences

Abilities, constraints, tastes, and personal circumstances.

Life experience

Past choices and events shape expectations and habits.

Functional needs

Customers use the same category to solve different problems.

Identity

Choices can express status, belonging, or self-image.

Marketing activity

Information and persuasion can make differences more visible.

Latent heterogeneity

Unobserved does not mean absent

Customers may have different preferences even when the market offers no way to express them.

  • Regulation or patents restrict choice.
  • Serving a small segment is too costly.
  • Technology cannot yet deliver the desired solution.
  • No firm has recognized the need.

Evolution of segmentation

Markets move toward smaller units

Mass marketing

One offering and one message for a broad market.

Niche marketing

Specialized offerings for narrower customer groups.

One-to-one marketing

Personalized decisions at the individual customer level.

Smaller segments improve customer fit, but they increase complexity and may reduce scale efficiency.

STP

Three connected decisions

Segmenting

Describe groups whose members seek similar benefits.

Targeting

Select groups where the firm can create and capture value.

Positioning

Establish a relevant advantage in the target customer's mind.

Segmentation variables

Needs define segments; descriptors help find them

Needs and benefits

What customers want the offering to accomplish. These variables should drive the segmentation.

Descriptors

Observable characteristics such as age, location, company size, or behavior. These variables help identify and reach segments.

A demographic group is not automatically a meaningful market segment.

Segmentation process

From customer language to segment profiles

  1. Use interviews and observation to identify possible purchase attributes.
  2. Survey a relevant sample of potential customers.
  3. Group related attributes when necessary.
  4. Group customers according to their needs.
  5. Describe, name, and assess the resulting segments.

Targeting

Attractiveness and competitive strength

Market attractiveness

Segment size, growth, willingness to pay, margins, and strategic importance.

Competitive strength

The firm's ability to meet the segment's needs better than competitors and sustain that position.

A large segment may be a poor target when the firm's advantage is weak.

Positioning

Who, what, and why

Who

Which customer or segment should the firm serve?

What

Which need or benefit should the offering fulfill?

Why

Why is this offering a better choice than the alternatives?

The positioning statement guides product, price, communication, channel, and service choices.

Takeaways

Good segmentation changes decisions

  • Customer differences matter when they imply different value propositions or actions.
  • Needs should define segments; descriptors should help the firm identify and reach them.
  • Targeting combines market opportunity with the firm's relative strength.
  • Positioning expresses a specific advantage for a specific target.
