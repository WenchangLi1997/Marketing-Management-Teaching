Marketing Principle #1

All customers differ

Customers vary in their needs, preferences, constraints, purchase behavior, and responses to marketing activity.

Customer heterogeneity

  • Customer heterogeneity means variation among customers in what they need, desire, value, and eventually choose.
  • A firm cannot serve every possible preference equally well.
  • Competitors often enter by serving a dissatisfied subgroup better than the incumbent.
  • The managerial problem is to decide which differences justify a different offer, message, channel, price, or service model.

A difference becomes strategically relevant when it changes what the firm should do.

  • Compare your answers with two classmates.
  • Could one retailer satisfy all three sets of preferences equally well?
  • Which segment does each retailer appear to target?
  • How would you describe the target customers of Walmart and Nordstrom?

Five sources of customer heterogeneity

  • Individual differences: resources, abilities, tastes, risk tolerance, and constraints.
  • Life experiences: prior choices and events shape expectations and habits.
  • Functional needs: customers use the same category to solve different problems.
  • Self-identity: purchases can communicate status, values, belonging, or self-image.
  • Marketing activity: information and persuasion influence which benefits customers notice and value.

Latent customer heterogeneity

Different preferences can exist even when current choices do not reveal them.

  • Legal constraints: regulation, licenses, or patents restrict available options.
  • Economic constraints: the segment is too small or costly to serve profitably.
  • Technological constraints: the desired solution cannot yet be produced.
  • Innovation constraints: no firm has identified and addressed the need.

Observed purchasing behavior may understate the diversity of customer demand.

Sears and the cost of serving everyone

  • Sears’ “Big Book” once offered a broad assortment intended to provide something for nearly everyone.
  • Specialists gradually identified attractive subgroups and designed more focused offers for them.
  • The broad-market incumbent retained a more diverse mix of slower-growing and less profitable customers.
  • The case illustrates a recurring risk: competitors can remove the most attractive niches from a mass-market business.

AT&T and newly visible preferences

  • Regulation can conceal heterogeneity by preventing competitors from offering alternatives.
  • After the breakup of the Bell System, competitors entered with offers aimed at different customer needs.
  • Demand that had appeared uniform became visibly segmented once customers gained meaningful choice.
  • Market structure can therefore determine whether customer differences remain latent or become observable.

From mass markets to individuals

Mass marketing

One broad offer and one message. Scale efficiencies matter more than precise fit.

Niche marketing

Specialized offers for narrower groups whose needs are more similar.

One-to-one marketing

Customer-level data and technology support personalized offers or recommendations.

The recurring trade-off is better customer fit versus the cost and complexity of customization.

Spotify and one-to-one marketing

  • Listening history provides behavioral evidence about individual preferences.
  • Personalized playlists reduce the effort required to discover relevant content.
  • The same preference data also help advertisers reach narrower audiences.
  • Personalization works when the additional relevance exceeds the data, technology, and coordination costs.

Core framework

Segmenting, targeting, and positioning

STP converts customer differences into a choice of whom to serve and how to create a relative advantage.

Three connected decisions

Segmenting

Divide the market into groups whose members have similar needs and whose needs differ across groups.

Targeting

Select the groups where the firm can create and capture value better than competitors.

Positioning

Build a clear relative advantage in the minds of the chosen customers.

What should define a segment?

Needs and benefits

  • What customers want the offering to accomplish
  • Benefits that matter in the purchase decision
  • Primary inputs to the segmentation analysis

Descriptors

  • Age, income, location, firm size, and observable behavior
  • Useful for identifying, reaching, and describing segments
  • Weak foundations when they do not explain different needs

A demographic category is not automatically a market segment.

Segmentation research process

  1. Define the category and the relevant population of potential customers.
  2. Use interviews, observation, and prior research to identify purchase attributes.
  3. Survey a relevant sample about the importance of needs and benefits.
  4. Combine highly related questions when several items measure the same underlying need.
  5. Group customers by similarity in their needs, then describe and name the groups.
  6. Assess whether the segments are identifiable, sustainable, manageable, and financially useful.

Useful segment profiles

  • Explain the needs that distinguish the group from other customers.
  • Report the group’s size, growth potential, willingness to pay, and expected economics.
  • Use descriptors to show how the firm can identify and reach prospective members.
  • Connect each profile to a different value proposition or marketing action.
  • Use memorable names only after the analysis establishes the group’s business meaning.

Targeting uses the 3Cs

Customers

Need intensity, segment size, growth, willingness to pay, and service requirements.

Company

Capabilities, resources, brand meaning, channels, economics, and implementation constraints.

Competitors

Existing offers, relative strengths, likely responses, and unserved customer needs.

Attractiveness describes the opportunity. Competitive strength describes the firm’s ability to capture it.

Six criteria for a target segment

  • The segment is based on a meaningful customer need.
  • Its needs differ enough from other groups to justify a distinct approach.
  • The firm’s capabilities match the segment’s requirements.
  • The opportunity is sustainable rather than temporary or easily copied.
  • Prospective customers can be identified and reached.
  • The expected long-term value exceeds the cost of acquisition and service.

The GE matrix for target selection

  • The vertical axis summarizes market attractiveness, often using size, growth, price sensitivity, and willingness to pay.
  • The horizontal axis summarizes competitive strength, including need fit, relative advantage, and execution capability.
  • Bubble size can represent segment size or expected economic value.
  • The matrix organizes judgment. It does not replace assumptions about weights, data quality, or competitor response.

Positioning

  • Positioning defines the relative advantage the firm wants target customers to associate with its offer.
  • The firm can change the actual offer through product, service, channel, or price decisions.
  • The firm can change perceptions through communication, evidence, branding, and customer experience.
  • Every element of the marketing program should support the intended position.

A position exists relative to competitors and substitutes, not in isolation.

Positioning statement

Who

Which customer or segment should the firm serve?

What

Which need or benefit should the offering fulfill?

Why

Why is the offering a better option than relevant competitors or substitutes?

For [target customer], our offer provides [benefit] because [credible support for relative advantage].

Beyond a one-time analysis

Customer centricity

A customer-centric firm places customer needs at the center of strategy, structure, information flows, and performance measures.

Market orientation in practice

  • Intelligence generation: employees regularly learn about current and emerging customer needs.
  • Intelligence dissemination: customer knowledge moves across functions instead of remaining in marketing or sales.
  • Responsiveness: the organization changes decisions and processes when the evidence warrants action.
  • Customer councils, frontline exposure, shared dashboards, and customer-based metrics can support these activities.

Customer-centric change takes time

  • Moving from product-based units to customer-focused units changes ownership, incentives, and coordination.
  • Performance may initially decline while employees learn new roles and the firm integrates customer information.
  • The potential payoff arrives after the organization develops richer knowledge and coordinates around priority segments.
  • STP creates external alignment with chosen customers. Customer centricity creates internal alignment around those customers.

DentMax: managing customer heterogeneity

  • DentMax leads the digital dental-imaging market on image quality and diagnostic flexibility.
  • Despite these strengths, the company lost market share and needed a clearer view of differences among dentists.
  • The analysis combined trade-show RFID behavior, prior purchases, product needs, and trusted information sources.
  • Cluster analysis produced four groups with different needs and commercial potential.

Week 2 recap

  • Customer differences threaten broad offers when competitors can serve attractive niches better.
  • Needs and benefits should define segments. Descriptors help identify and reach their members.
  • Targeting combines market attractiveness with the firm’s competitive strength.
  • Positioning specifies a credible relative advantage for a chosen target.
  • Customer centricity helps the organization keep learning and responding after the initial STP analysis.
